Fixed tilt: panels are bolted at one angle, facing south, and never move. This was cheap and standard before about 2016. Tracking: rows of panels rotate east to west during the day to follow the sun, producing about 15–25% more energy from the same panels. Fixed-tilt sites have the most to gain from repowering, because new trackers can be added behind the existing interconnection.

    About this map

    This map points to existing US solar sites where swapping in modern modules, trackers and inverters could produce a lot more energy without a new interconnection request. The idea comes from Foundry-Logic and Crux's repowering study (Heatmap coverage). The study estimates 9.6 GW of repowering potential by 2030 and 67 GW by 2040, and finds that newer panels produce up to 70% more energy than old ones.

    Data

    Every operable utility-scale (≥1 MW) solar PV and battery generator in EIA Form 860 (), rolled up to the plant. Location, balancing authority, grid voltage, racking, panel technology, DC capacity and owners all come from EIA. The plant's commissioning year is the capacity-weighted average of its generators' years.

    Grid regions

    Each plant is assigned to a market by its EIA balancing authority, not by where it sits on the map. This matters at the edges. About 2.3 GW of 2010–16 solar in Arizona and Nevada (Agua Caliente and Silver State, among others) is dynamically scheduled into CAISO, so CAISO's interconnection rules apply even though the plants are outside California. LADWP and BANC plants are inside California but outside the CAISO market. The boundary shapes are simplified and only for display.

    Repowering waves

    Applying EIA build years to the study's totals (9.6 GW by 2030, 29 GW by 2035, 67 GW by 2040) suggests plants repower at about 15–18 years old. Fleet built through 2015: 12.4 GW. Through 2018: 30.7 GW. Through 2022: 72.3 GW. The 2030 wave is mostly CAISO and the non-RTO Southwest. MISO, which the study rates as having the most favorable repowering rules, has little old solar today (0.4 GW built before 2017) but added 16 GW in 2023–25, so its rules matter most for the 2040 wave.

    North Carolina contract cliff

    Before HB 589 (2017), NC let qualifying facilities (QFs) of up to 5 MW sign 15-year standard-offer contracts under PURPA, the federal law requiring utilities to buy power from small independent generators. Together with the state's 35% renewable tax credit, which expired at the end of 2015, this produced hundreds of fixed-tilt 5 MW farms. The NC view shows every NC solar plant that EIA flags as a QF, with its contract assumed to end 15 years after it entered service. On that basis, 1.45 GW expires in 2026–30 and 2.15 GW in 2026–31, which brackets Duke's 1.9 GW five-year figure. Actual contract dates vary: some sites signed before going into service, and some renewed once under HB 951 (2021). The battery requirement is 20% of AC nameplate for 4 hours, following Duke's 2026 proposal. Operators are as reported to EIA and are usually the asset manager; legal owners are often project-level LLCs.

    Estimated uplift

    The AC interconnection limit is held constant. The 70% figure is about energy (MWh), not capacity (MW). The uplift for each plant multiplies four effects:

    • Degradation recovered: about 0.7% of output lost per year of age, regained with new modules
    • Tracking: +20% for capacity that is currently fixed-tilt
    • Bifacial modules: +5%
    • Higher DC/AC ratio: raising the plant's reported ratio to 1.35, with 60% of the added DC turning into energy after clipping

    The result is capped at 70%. Added TWh uses the plant's AC capacity and an assumed capacity factor (20% fixed, 24% tracking). These are screening numbers for comparing sites, not an engineering assessment. The next step would be to replace them with actual EIA-923 generation and site-specific irradiance.
